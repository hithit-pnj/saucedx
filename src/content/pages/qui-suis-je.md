  texte:
    - I studied philosophy because the questions I carried never found anyone to
      meet them. I became a teacher because I wanted to see what those questions
      do to people when they're given time.
    - "What I learnt in the classroom is that philosophy does nothing for you as
      long as it stays mere knowledge, and changes everything once it becomes an
      interaction. "
    - "So far, I've always found what I was looking for, intellectually, in what
      had already been written. And since research, by definition, begins where
      existing texts leave off, I've never had to take that road (not yet,
      anyway). I'm not in the research sauce. And that's exactly what I can
      offer those who are: a clear head from outside, and a helping hand from
      someone who knows your field and its methods. We speak the same language,
      just not the same tongue. So my explanations follow the way you think, and
      draw on the kinds of reasoning you already trust."
    - "Frenchy Sauce grew out of that. English-speaking researchers who spend
      (some of) their time with French thinkers, often alone with a translation
      and a dictionary. French is my native language, philosophy is my trade,
      and twelve years in hospitality taught me that French lives around a
      dinner table as much as in a seminar room. So I offer the interlocutor I
      would have wanted to find: someone who reads the texts with you, pushes,
      insists, and is never in a hurry to move on."
    - "I know what it is to work on thoughts that were thought in another
      language. During my master's, my sauce was German: Kant and Freud, through
      translations, with a bilingual edition when there was one and the Pons
      dictionary open, on my own. I could never shake the feeling that a German
      reader saw something I didn't. With French thought, the original is in my
      language: I can be the someone I didn't have."
